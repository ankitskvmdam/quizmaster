## Gist
*Book pages 13–20.*

Studying a society's history starts with its **historiography — the study of historical interpretation**. The book reads modern Indian history under four broad approaches — **Colonial (Imperialist), Nationalist, Marxist and Subaltern** — plus other influential ones: **Communalist, Cambridge, Liberal/Neo-liberal and Feminist**.

The **Colonial School** dominated for most of the **19th century**. Written by colonial officials to justify domination, it criticised indigenous society and praised Western values. Examples: **James Mill, Mountstuart Elphinstone, Vincent Smith**. Its features: an **'Orientalist' representation** of India; the claim that **the British brought unity**; **Social Darwinism** (the English as the fittest to rule); India as a **stagnant society needing British guidance ('White Man's burden')**; and **Pax Britannica** to bring law and order.

The **Nationalist approach** grew **as a response to and in confrontation with** the colonial approach and sought to unify people across religion, caste, language and class. **Nationalist historians of modern India did not exist before 1947**; before then nationalist history dealt mainly with ancient and medieval India. In the **last quarter of the 19th century**, **Dadabhai Naoroji, M.G. Ranade, G.V. Joshi, R.C. Dutt, K.T. Telang, G.K. Gokhale and D.E. Wacha** built an economic critique of colonialism. Accounts of the national movement came from leaders (not historians) such as **R.G. Pradhan, A.C. Mazumdar, J.L. Nehru and Pattabhi Sitaramayya**. **R.C. Majumdar and Tara Chand** are noted nationalist historians.

The **Marxist approach** began with **Rajni Palme Dutt's *India Today*** (written for the **Left Book Club**; published in England **1940**, in India **1947**) and **A.R. Desai's *Social Background of Indian Nationalism* (1948)**. Marxists see the **primary contradiction between colonial masters and subject people**, and also the **inner contradictions** within Indian society, but tend to treat the national movement as a **bourgeois movement**. **Sumit Sarkar** called Dutt's paradigm a **"simplistic version of the Marxian class approach"**. **A.R. Desai** traced the movement in **five phases**.

The **Subaltern school** began in the **early 1980s** under **Ranajit Guha**. It holds that all Indian historiography had an **elitist bias** and that the basic contradiction was between the **elite (Indian and foreign) and subaltern groups**. It rejects a class-only reading because **capitalism was then nascent**, and sees two streams: the real anti-imperialist stream of the subalterns and the 'bogus' elite national movement. **Bipan Chandra** criticised this trend.

The **Communalist approach** treated **Hindus and Muslims as permanently hostile groups** and medieval history as one long Hindu-Muslim conflict, a view that ended in riots and partition. The **Cambridge School** held that the main contradiction was **among Indians themselves**, with leaders seeking **power and material benefits** — criticised as reducing nationalism to **'animal politics'**. **Liberal/Neo-liberal** writers (**Patrick O'Brian, Hopkins and Cain**) argue colonial exploitation did not benefit the British people as a whole. **Feminist historiography** grew from the **women's movement of the 1970s**; colonial-era works that drew international attention were **Pandita Ramabai's *The High Caste Hindu Woman* (1887)** and **Katherine Mayo's *Mother India* (1927)**.

## Key facts
- **Historiography** — the study of historical interpretation.
- Four broad approaches: **Colonial (Imperialist), Nationalist, Marxist, Subaltern**; others: **Communalist, Cambridge, Liberal/Neo-liberal, Feminist**.
- Colonial School dominated for the major part of the **19th century**; examples **James Mill, Mountstuart Elphinstone, Vincent Smith**.
- Colonial approach features: **Orientalist representation**, **British brought unity**, **Social Darwinism**, **stagnant India / White Man's burden**, **Pax Britannica**.
- The term 'colonial approach' is used today mainly for **works influenced by the colonial ideology of domination**.
- Nationalist approach developed **as a response to and in confrontation with the colonial approach**.
- **Nationalist historians of modern India did not exist before 1947**; earlier nationalist history dealt with **ancient and medieval** periods.
- Economic critique of colonialism (last quarter of 19th century): **Dadabhai Naoroji, M.G. Ranade, G.V. Joshi, R.C. Dutt, K.T. Telang, G.K. Gokhale, D.E. Wacha**.
- Accounts of the national movement by leaders (not historians): **R.G. Pradhan, A.C. Mazumdar, J.L. Nehru, Pattabhi Sitaramayya**.
- Noted nationalist historians of modern India: **R.C. Majumdar and Tara Chand**.
- ***India Today*** — **Rajni Palme Dutt**; written for the **Left Book Club**, published in England **1940**, in India **1947**.
- ***Social Background of Indian Nationalism*** — **A.R. Desai**, first published **1948**; Desai traced the national movement in **five phases**.
- **Sumit Sarkar** called R.P. Dutt's paradigm a **"simplistic version of the Marxian class approach"**.
- Subaltern school began in the **early 1980s** under the editorship of **Ranajit Guha**.
- Subaltern view: basic contradiction between the **elite (Indian and foreign) and subaltern groups**, not between colonialism and the Indian people.
- **Bipan Chandra** criticised the subaltern trend for dismissing all earlier writing as 'elite historiography'.
- Communalist approach: **Hindus and Muslims as permanent hostile groups**; relied on colonial historiography of medieval India.
- **Cambridge School**: fundamental contradiction **among Indians themselves**; criticised for reducing nationalism to **'animal politics'**.
- Liberal/Neo-liberal proponents: **Patrick O'Brian, Hopkins and Cain**.
- Feminist historiography began with the **women's movement of the 1970s**, later broadened into **gender history**.
- ***The High Caste Hindu Woman* (1887)** — **Pandita Ramabai**; ***Mother India* (1927)** — **Katherine Mayo**.
- **Percival Spear**: histories of India call for reinterpretation because of **changes in the Indian scene** and **changes in historians' attitudes**.
