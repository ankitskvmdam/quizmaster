## At a glance: the three stages of colonialism

Rajni Palme Dutt divided colonialism in India into three stages.

| Stage | Period | Name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First stage | 1757–1813 | Merchant Capital |
| Second stage | 1813–1860s | Colonialism of Free Trade |
| Third stage | From around the 1860s | Era of Foreign Investments and International Competition for Colonies |

## Background: a colonial economy

1. The British were unlike the earlier invaders. They made structural changes in the Indian economy.
2. They drained the wealth of India as tribute.
3. So India became a colonial economy, shaped by British interests.
4. India had about 23 per cent of the world economy at the start of the 18th century. At independence it had only about 3 per cent.

## Deindustrialisation

1. **1813:** The Charter Act of 1813 allowed one-way free trade for British citizens.
2. Tariffs of nearly 80 per cent were put on Indian textiles.
3. **After 1820:** European markets were virtually closed to Indian exports.
4. India turned from a net exporter into a net importer.
5. The ruined artisans moved to the villages. This was called ruralisation. It added to the pressure on land.
6. Shashi Tharoor notes in An Era of Darkness that India's share of world manufacturing exports fell from 27 per cent to 2 per cent.

Two remarks on the ruin of the artisans:

- **William Bentinck:** "Bones of cotton weavers are bleaching the plains."
- **D.H. Buchanan:** Buchanan spoke of the "steel rail" piercing the village.

## Peasants and land

- **Permanent Settlement:** Land became transferable under it. This made the tenants insecure. The zamindars used evictions, illegal dues and begar (forced unpaid labour).
- **Triple burden:** The peasant bore a triple burden of the Government, the zamindar and the money-lender. This was so in zamindari, Ryotwari and Mahalwari areas alike.
- **By 1815:** Half of the land in Bengal had passed into new hands. The new owners were merchants and moneylenders. This gave rise to sub-infeudation and absentee landlordism.
- **1850 to 1900:** About 2.8 crore people died in famines between these years.

## Commercialisation of agriculture

1. Commercialisation of agriculture came in the latter half of the 19th century.
2. It reached its highest level in the plantation sector. The plantations grew tea, coffee, rubber and indigo. They were mostly European-owned.
3. **1860s:** The cotton boom helped the intermediaries.
4. **1866:** Cotton prices slumped. The slump hit the cultivators. It brought indebtedness and famine.
5. **1870s:** Agrarian riots broke out in the Deccan.

## Industry

### Ship-building

- **Centres:** Surat and Malabar were the centres in the west. Bengal and Masulipatnam were the centres in the east.
- **1813:** A law barred ships below 350 tonnes from the trade between India and Britain.
- **1814:** A law said that Indian-built ships would not be treated as 'British-registered vessels'.
- These two laws crushed Indian ship-building.

### Modern industry

- **1853:** The first cotton textile mill was set up at Bombay by Cowasjee Nanabhoy.
- **1855:** The first jute mill was set up at Rishra in Bengal.
- **Foreign ownership:** Most modern industry was foreign-owned and run under managing agencies.
- **Foreign capital:** Indian tea, jute and manganese had ready markets abroad. This attracted foreign capital.
- **Indian-owned industries:** In the 19th century these were cotton textiles and jute. In the 20th century they were sugar and cement.
- **Tatas:** They were forced to produce a higher standard of steel for British use. So they lost the larger market.

## Nationalist critique of the colonial economy

### The critics

Disillusionment set in after the 1860s.

- **Dadabhai Naoroji:** He was called the 'Grand Old Man of India'. He put forward the drain theory in his book Poverty and UnBritish Rule in India.
- **R.C. Dutt:** He wrote The Economic History of India.
- **G.V. Joshi:** He called the "expenditure on railways ... an Indian subsidy to British industries".
- **Other critics:** M.G. Ranade, Gokhale, G. Subramaniya Iyer and Prithwishchandra Ray.

### The drain of wealth

The drain had five components:

1. Salaries and pensions.
2. Interest on foreign loans.
3. Profits on foreign investment.
4. Stores bought in Britain.
5. Payments for shipping, banking and insurance.

The drain was estimated in three ways:

- more than the total land revenue;
- half of the total government revenue;
- one-third of the total savings, which was 8 per cent of the national product.

Three remarks on the drain:

- **John Sullivan, President of the Board of Revenue, Madras:** He spoke of a "sponge" that draws from the Ganges and squeezes on the Thames.
- **Sir George Wingate:** He said taxes spent abroad "might as well be thrown into the sea".
- **Angus Maddison:** He said the outflow "lasted for 190 years".

### Nationalist demands

1. Reduce the land revenue.
2. Abolish the salt tax.
3. Impose income tax.
4. Impose excise duties on the goods of the rich.

This agitation fed the moderate phase (1875–1905), the seed-time of the national movement. The years are as given in this chapter.

## The three stages: one by one

### First stage (1757–1813): Merchant Capital

- This was the stage of monopoly trade and direct appropriation of revenue.
- The drain was 2–3 per cent of Britain's national income.

### Second stage (1813–1860s): Colonialism of Free Trade

- India became a market for British goods and a supplier of raw materials.
- India absorbed 10–12 per cent of British exports.
- It absorbed nearly 20 per cent of Britain's textile exports.

### Third stage (from around the 1860s): Era of Foreign Investments and International Competition for Colonies

- **Viceroys:** This stage saw the reactionary rule of Lytton, Dufferin, Lansdowne and Curzon.
- **Trusteeship:** The British now spoke of a permanent 'trusteeship' and the 'White Man's burden'.
- **Self-government:** The notion of training Indians for self-government vanished. It was revived only after 1918.
- **Sachidanand Sinha of Bihar:** He said the Government was run to the dictates of the Chamber of Commerce. For him this was the 'White Man's Burden'.
