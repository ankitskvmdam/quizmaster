## Gist
*Book pages 541–556.*

Unlike earlier invaders, the British made **structural changes** in the Indian economy and drained its wealth as tribute, turning it into a **colonial economy** shaped by British interests. India had about **23 per cent of the world economy** at the start of the 18th century and only about **3 per cent** at independence.

**Deindustrialisation:** the **Charter Act of 1813** allowed **one-way free trade** for British citizens; tariffs of nearly **80 per cent** were put on Indian textiles, and **after 1820** European markets were virtually closed to Indian exports. India went from **net exporter to net importer**. Ruined artisans moved to villages (**ruralisation**), adding to pressure on land. Shashi Tharoor notes India's share of world manufacturing exports fell from **27 to 2 per cent**.

**Peasants and land:** under the **Permanent Settlement**, transferability of land made tenants insecure; zamindars used evictions, illegal dues and **begar**. The peasant bore a **triple burden — Government, zamindar and money-lender** — in zamindari, **Ryotwari** and **Mahalwari** areas alike. **By 1815, half the land in Bengal** had passed to new hands, giving rise to **sub-infeudation** and **absentee landlordism**. Between **1850 and 1900 about 2.8 crore people died in famines**.

**Commercialisation** of agriculture (latter half of the 19th century) peaked in the **plantation sector** (tea, coffee, rubber, indigo), mostly European-owned. The cotton boom of the 1860s helped intermediaries; the **1866 slump** hit cultivators, leading to **agrarian riots in the Deccan in the 1870s**.

**Industry:** ship-building at **Surat, Malabar, Bengal and Masulipatnam** was crushed by an **1813 law** (ships **below 350 tonnes** barred from the India–Britain route) and an **1814 law** (Indian-built ships not 'British-registered'). The **first cotton mill (1853, Bombay, Cowasjee Nanabhoy)** and **first jute mill (1855, Rishra, Bengal)** came up, but most modern industry was foreign-owned under **managing agencies**.

**Nationalist critique:** disillusionment set in **after the 1860s**. **Dadabhai Naoroji** ('Grand Old Man of India') put forward the **drain theory** in **Poverty and UnBritish Rule in India**; others were **M.G. Ranade, R.C. Dutt (The Economic History of India), Gokhale, G. Subramaniya Iyer and Prithwishchandra Ray**. The drain was estimated at **more than total land revenue, half of government revenue, or one-third of total savings (8 per cent of national product)**. **G.V. Joshi** called railway expenditure 'an Indian subsidy to British industries'. This agitation fed the **moderate phase (1875–1905)**.

**Stages of colonialism (Rajni Palme Dutt):** (1) **Merchant capital, 1757–1813** — monopoly trade and direct appropriation of revenue; drain = **2–3 per cent of Britain's national income**; (2) **Colonialism of Free Trade, 1813–1860s** — India a market for British goods and supplier of raw materials; (3) **Era of Foreign Investments and International Competition for Colonies, from around the 1860s** — reactionary rule of **Lytton, Dufferin, Lansdowne and Curzon**, permanent 'trusteeship' and the **'White Man's burden'**. Bihar link: **Sachidanand Sinha** is quoted on the Government serving the Chamber of Commerce.

## Key facts
- India's share of world economy: about **23 per cent** (early 18th century) → about **3 per cent** at independence.
- **Charter Act of 1813** allowed **one-way free trade** for British citizens.
- Tariffs of nearly **80 per cent** on Indian textiles; European markets virtually closed **after 1820**.
- "Bones of cotton weavers are bleaching the plains" — **William Bentinck**; "steel rail" piercing the village — **D.H. Buchanan**.
- India's share of world manufacturing exports fell from **27 per cent to 2 per cent** (**Shashi Tharoor, An Era of Darkness**).
- Peasant's **triple burden**: **Government, zamindar and money-lender**.
- **By 1815**, **half of the land in Bengal** had passed into new hands (merchants, moneylenders).
- Between **1850 and 1900**, about **2.8 crore** people died in famines.
- Commercialisation reached its highest level in the **plantation sector** — tea, coffee, rubber, indigo, mostly **European-owned**.
- Cotton price slump of **1866** → indebtedness, famine and **agrarian riots in the Deccan (1870s)**.
- Ship-building centres: **Surat, Malabar** (west); **Bengal, Masulipatnam** (east).
- **1813** law: ships **below 350 tonnes** barred from India–Britain trade; **1814** law: Indian-built ships not treated as **'British-registered vessels'**.
- **Tatas** were forced to produce a **higher standard of steel** for British use, losing the larger market.
- First cotton textile mill: **1853, Bombay, Cowasjee Nanabhoy**; first jute mill: **1855, Rishra (Bengal)**.
- Ready markets abroad for Indian **tea, jute and manganese** attracted foreign capital.
- Indian-owned industries: **cotton textiles and jute** (19th century); **sugar, cement** (20th century).
- Drain theory — **Dadabhai Naoroji**, **Poverty and UnBritish Rule in India**; **The Economic History of India** — **R.C. Dutt**.
- Drain components: **salaries and pensions**, **interest on foreign loans**, **profits on foreign investment**, **stores bought in Britain**, **shipping, banking and insurance payments**.
- Drain estimates: **more than total land revenue**, **half of total government revenue**, **one-third of total savings** (**8 per cent of national product**).
- "Expenditure on railways ... an Indian subsidy to British industries" — **G.V. Joshi**.
- Nationalist demands: **reduce land revenue**, **abolish salt tax**, **impose income tax** and **excise duties** on goods of the rich.
- "Sponge" drawing from the **Ganges** and squeezing on the **Thames** — **John Sullivan**, President, Board of Revenue, **Madras**.
- Taxes spent abroad "might as well be thrown into the sea" — **Sir George Wingate**; outflow "lasted for 190 years" — **Angus Maddison**.
- Government run to the dictates of the Chamber of Commerce = the 'White Man's Burden' — **Sachidanand Sinha** (Bihar).
- Moderate phase (seed-time of the national movement): **1875–1905** (as given in this chapter).
- Three stages of colonialism — **Rajni Palme Dutt**.
- **First stage (1757–1813)**: Merchant Capital / Monopoly Trade and Direct Appropriation; drain = **2–3 per cent of Britain's national income**.
- **Second stage (1813–1860s)**: **Colonialism of Free Trade**; India absorbed **10–12 per cent** of British exports and nearly **20 per cent** of its textile exports.
- **Third stage (from around 1860s)**: **Era of Foreign Investments and International Competition for Colonies**; viceroys **Lytton, Dufferin, Lansdowne, Curzon**.
- Notion of training Indians for self-government vanished in the third stage, **revived only after 1918**.
